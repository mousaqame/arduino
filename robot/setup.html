2

Wire it up

There are two separate jobs here. The signal wires tell each motor where to move, and the power wires feed them. Doing them one at a time is much easier than trying to do both at once.

Do not power the motors from the NodeMCU

Four motors pull more current than the NodeMCU can supply. If you connect their red wires to the board, it restarts the moment the robot moves — and it looks like a broken WiFi problem, not a power one. They need their own 5V adapter.

Part one — the signal wires

Each motor has three wires. The orange one is the signal. Each goes to its own pin on the NodeMCU.

These four pins were chosen on purpose. Most other pins on the NodeMCU have to be at a certain voltage while the board starts up, and a connected motor pulls them the wrong way — so the board won't switch on at all.

Part two — the power wires

The other two wires on every motor are power. Build two rows on your breadboard: a 5V row and a GND row. Everything connects to those.

A 5V adapter with the cable cut back to bare wires. Steady and never runs out, but the robot stays near a socket. The amps matter more than the volts.

Under about 1.5 amps? Switch on low-power mode

One MG90S motor pulls around 0.7 A on its own when it's straining, and four moving together want 1.2–1.6 A. A smaller supply can't feed them all at once — they'll jitter, stall, or just stop.

The robot has a Low-power mode button on its control page for exactly this. The motors take turns instead of moving together, they move a little slower, and they let go once everything has settled. Moves look less lively but they work properly, and nothing gets damaged.

Most phone chargers made in the last few years do 2 A or more — check the small print on the plug. A USB power bank is usually 2 A too, and makes the robot portable at the same time.

Using a power bank? It may switch itself off

Power banks are built to stop draining once a phone finishes charging, so most shut down when they see less than about 100 mA for a while. A robot sitting still can easily draw less than that — so it works perfectly, you leave it alone for a minute, and the motors go dead.

If that happens: many banks have a low-current or always-on mode, usually a quick double-press of the button — check the manual. Otherwise just avoid leaving it idle, and be aware that low-power mode makes this more likely, because it releases the motors after a few seconds and the draw drops to almost nothing.

Two USB sockets on the bank? Run the motors from one and the NodeMCU's own cable from the other, and the robot is completely wireless. Both sockets already share a ground inside the bank, so that part is taken care of — but check whether the 2 A is per socket or shared between them.

Use rechargeable NiMH, not alkaline

4 × AA NiMH rechargeables (Eneloop and similar) give 4.8V, right in the middle of what these motors want. Just as importantly they can deliver current in big gulps without their voltage collapsing — which is exactly what starting a motor demands.

Ordinary alkaline AAs look better on paper — 4 of them give 6V and they hold more charge. In this robot they disappoint. Alkalines have high internal resistance, so the moment four motors pull current the pack's voltage drops like a stone, and the robot stutters or resets. They also empty far faster than their rating suggests when drained hard. They will work for a short while if it's all you have; they are not the thing to build around.

PackVoltageVerdict
4 × AA NiMH4.8 V Best choice. Roughly 2–4 hours of play, then recharge.

9V block9 V No. Too much voltage, and it can't supply nearly enough current.
The NodeMCU keeps its own power

Don't run the NodeMCU from the battery pack. When the motors pull hard the pack dips, and the NodeMCU restarts — the exact fault we're avoiding. Leave it on its USB cable. For a robot that roams free, plug that cable into a small USB power bank. Two supplies, one shared GND row.

Get a 4-cell AA holder with a switch — it saves unplugging things constantly. Never mix old and new cells, or different brands, in the same pack. The 1000 µF capacitor matters more with batteries than with an adapter, not less.

The wire everyone forgets

That last row. The NodeMCU's GND must join the same GND row as the motors. Without it the motors have nothing to measure the signal against, so they twitch randomly or sit still — even though every other wire is perfect.

The capacitor only goes in one way round. The leg next to the stripe is the negative one and goes to the GND row. It smooths out the sudden gulps of current the motors take when they start moving, which is what stops the board resetting.
